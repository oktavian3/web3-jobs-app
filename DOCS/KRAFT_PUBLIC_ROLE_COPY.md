## 1. Community Moderator

> Maintains safe, usable community channels by enforcing rules, answering routine questions, spotting scams, and escalating issues that require product, security, or leadership decisions.

### What this role actually does

Most days, the practitioner has to review handoff notes, scan Discord and Telegram, answer routine questions, remove obvious abuse, and document anything that crosses an escalation threshold.

Its decision rights usually cover channel safety, rule enforcement, and routine support triage, while community strategy and partnerships remain outside the default remit.

### Where the role sits

Community Moderator usually sits inside Community, Growth, Ecosystem, or regional market teams. Common reporting lines include Head of Community, Growth Lead, Ecosystem Lead, or a regional market lead. Full-time core-team roles exist, but regional contracts, agencies, contributor programmes, and part-time coverage are common. The role usually collaborates with Community Manager, Customer Support Specialist, Operations Associate.

### Core responsibilities

- Monitor public channels and remove scams, impersonation, spam, harassment, and coordinated disruption.
- Answer recurring questions from approved sources and avoid improvising policy.
- Record product, payment, security, and sentiment issues in an escalation system.
- Keep pinned messages, FAQ references, and moderation notes current.
- Support launches, AMAs, governance votes, and incidents with higher coverage.
- Apply rules consistently across languages, time zones, and high-status members.

### Daily, weekly, and reactive work

**Typical day:** Review handoff notes, scan Discord and Telegram, answer routine questions, remove obvious abuse, and document anything that crosses an escalation threshold.

**Weekly or monthly cadence:** Review incident patterns, update FAQ and macros, join moderator syncs, and flag recurring issues that need product or policy fixes.

**When conditions change:** Respond to phishing waves, impersonation, raids, exploit rumors, service outages, or reward disputes without amplifying unverified claims.

### Deliverables

- Moderation log.
- Escalation ticket.
- Incident summary.
- Updated FAQ or macro.
- Shift handoff note.

### How success is judged

- Fast removal of harmful content.
- Accurate escalation.
- Consistent rule application.
- Fewer repeated unresolved incidents.

Read fast removal of harmful content together with accurate escalation. Neither signal is meaningful without the relevant launch, incident, market, workload, or attribution context.

### Tools in practice

- **Discord:** Monitor reports, apply channel rules, answer from approved sources, and leave a clean handoff for the next shift.
- **Telegram:** Monitor reports, apply channel rules, answer from approved sources, and leave a clean handoff for the next shift.
- **Notion:** Draft, review, and maintain moderation log and escalation ticket, with owners, source links, and change history.
- **Google Sheets:** Structure the records behind moderation log and escalation ticket and make review status visible.
- **Zendesk or Linear:** Track decisions, dependencies, bugs, and owners connected to moderation log and escalation ticket; the ticket is a coordination record, not the work itself.

### Skills and prerequisite knowledge

**Hard skills**

- Scam-pattern recognition.
- Wallet and transaction literacy.
- Moderation tooling.
- Incident documentation.
- Basic support triage.

**Working skills**

- Clear public communication.
- Calm judgment under pressure.
- Cross-cultural awareness.
- Follow-through.
- Stakeholder expectation management.

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Know common wallet scams, project rules, escalation contacts, and the difference between confirmed information and rumor.

### Expectations by level

**Entry level:** At entry level, a candidate should be able to complete a scoped assignment with review. That includes the ability to monitor public channels and remove scams, impersonation, spam, harassment, and coordinated disruption, to answer recurring questions from approved sources and avoid improvising policy, and to produce reviewable artifacts such as a moderation log and an escalation ticket.

**Mid level:** At mid level, the practitioner normally owns channel safety, rule enforcement, and routine support triage without constant supervision. They can coordinate adjacent teams and improve the workflow behind a moderation log and an escalation ticket, including when the role must respond to phishing waves, impersonation, raids, exploit rumors, service outages, or reward disputes without amplifying unverified claims.

**Senior:** At senior level, the work shifts toward standards, decision rights, and review quality. A senior Community Moderator defines how channel safety, rule enforcement, and routine support triage are handled, reviews high-risk cases, and builds systems that do not depend on one person.

### Proof of work and portfolio

Reviewers should be able to inspect a moderation log and an escalation ticket, trace the inputs or decisions behind the work, and understand what the candidate personally owned.

**Strong examples**

- A moderation SOP with escalation levels.
- An anonymized incident log.
- A phishing-response flow.
- A launch coverage plan.

**Weak evidence**

- Screenshots showing high message volume with no explanation of decisions.
- Claims that being active in Discord equals moderation skill.
- Generic community growth charts.

### Common mistakes and misconceptions

**Common mistakes**

- Taking responsibility for community strategy and partnerships without the mandate or approval to do so.

**Common misconception**

Community Moderator may overlap with Community Manager, but the hiring evidence is different. This role is judged on channel safety, rule enforcement, and routine support triage, not on ownership of community strategy and partnerships.

### Scope boundaries

**Usually owns**

- Channel safety.
- Rule enforcement.
- Routine support triage.
- Incident logging.
- Escalation quality.

**Usually does not own**

- Community strategy.
- Partnerships.
- Creator contracting.
- Product roadmap decisions.
- Public crisis statements without approval.

### Interview focus

Expect questions about scam-pattern recognition, wallet and transaction literacy, and moderation tooling, plus a scenario where the role must respond to phishing waves, impersonation, raids, exploit rumors, service outages, or reward disputes without amplifying unverified claims. Interviewers are looking for evidence that the candidate knows where channel safety and rule enforcement stop and community strategy and partnerships begin.

**Example questions**

- A scam link appears during a token launch. What do you do in the first five minutes?
- When should a moderator answer directly, and when should the issue move to support, security, or leadership?
- How would you keep enforcement consistent across several moderators and time zones?

### Compensation and role risks

Direct role-specific salary evidence is limited because many moderator roles are shift-based, part-time, contributor, or contractor work.

**Role risks**

- Burnout from continuous public exposure.
- Abuse and harassment.
- Unclear off-hours expectations.
- Being given security responsibility without authority.

### Career path and role fit

**Common progression**

- Community Manager.
- Community Operations or Customer Support.
- Trust and Safety.

**This role may fit people who**

People who stay calm in noisy public channels, notice patterns quickly, and are comfortable enforcing rules without turning every interaction into a debate.

**This role may not fit people who**

People who want primarily strategic work, dislike reactive communication, or cannot maintain boundaries around shift coverage.

### Practical next steps

- Moderate a real community under clear supervision.
- Write an escalation matrix for scams, bugs, account issues, and policy disputes.
- Turn three anonymized incidents into short case studies explaining the decision and outcome.

## 2. Community Manager

> Owns community health, programming, feedback loops, moderation systems, and the relationship between users and the wider product or ecosystem team.

### What this role actually does

This is hands-on work. The role may need to review channel health, answer high-context questions, coordinate moderators, gather user feedback, and maintain the current communication calendar.

The boundary matters: it owns community strategy, programming cadence, and moderator oversight, not the product roadmap and all social-media publishing.

### Where the role sits

Community Manager usually sits inside Community, Growth, Ecosystem, or regional market teams. Common reporting lines include Head of Community, Growth Lead, Ecosystem Lead, or a regional market lead. Full-time core-team roles exist, but regional contracts, agencies, contributor programmes, and part-time coverage are common. The role usually collaborates with Community Moderator, Ambassador Manager, Growth Manager, Ecosystem Partnerships Manager.

### Core responsibilities

- Set channel strategy and define what each community space is for.
- Plan recurring programming such as office hours, AMAs, contributor calls, and local events.
- Manage moderators, local leads, and escalation workflows.
- Translate product updates into community communication and community signals into internal feedback.
- Track health, participation, retention, recurring complaints, and emerging risks.
- Coordinate with product, support, marketing, and ecosystem teams during launches and incidents.

### Daily, weekly, and reactive work

**Typical day:** Review channel health, answer high-context questions, coordinate moderators, gather user feedback, and maintain the current communication calendar.

**Weekly or monthly cadence:** Run programming, publish a health report, review escalations, meet product or growth teams, and update priorities for the next cycle.

**When conditions change:** Handle misinformation, exploit rumors, delayed rewards, unpopular policy changes, regional conflict, and sudden activity spikes.

### Deliverables

- Community strategy.
- Monthly programming calendar.
- Health and sentiment report.
- Feedback taxonomy.
- Crisis communication plan.
- Event recap.

### How success is judged

- Useful participation rather than raw message volume.
- Retention of credible contributors.
- Quality and speed of feedback escalation.
- Well-run events and fewer recurring unresolved issues.

Read useful participation rather than raw message volume together with retention of credible contributors. Neither signal is meaningful without the relevant launch, incident, market, workload, or attribution context.

### Tools in practice

- **Discord:** Run programming, coordinate moderators, collect recurring user signals, and manage incident communication.
- **Telegram:** Run programming, coordinate moderators, collect recurring user signals, and manage incident communication.
- **Notion:** Draft, review, and maintain community strategy and monthly programming calendar, with owners, source links, and change history.
- **Typeform:** Collect structured intake for community strategy and monthly programming calendar, with consent and ownership fields where needed.
- **Dune or product analytics:** Compare community signals with product or onchain behaviour to see whether participation turns into useful action.
- **event tools:** Manage registration, reminders, attendance, speaker details, and post-event feedback for recurring programmes.

### Skills and prerequisite knowledge

**Hard skills**

- Community programme design.
- Reporting and segmentation.
- Event operations.
- Moderation systems.
- Crypto product literacy.

**Working skills**

- Clear public communication.
- Calm judgment under pressure.
- Cross-cultural awareness.
- Follow-through.
- Stakeholder expectation management.

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Understand the product, target users, major risks, community channels, regional differences, and how to measure behavior beyond follower counts.

### Expectations by level

**Entry level:** At entry level, a candidate should be able to complete a scoped assignment with review. That includes the ability to set channel strategy and define what each community space is for, to plan recurring programming such as office hours, AMAs, contributor calls, and local events, and to produce reviewable artifacts such as a community strategy and a monthly programming calendar.

**Mid level:** At mid level, the practitioner normally owns community strategy, programming cadence, and moderator oversight without constant supervision. They can coordinate adjacent teams and improve the workflow behind a community strategy and a monthly programming calendar, including when the role must handle misinformation, exploit rumors, delayed rewards, unpopular policy changes, regional conflict, and sudden activity spikes.

**Senior:** At senior level, the work shifts toward standards, decision rights, and review quality. A senior Community Manager defines how community strategy, programming cadence, and moderator oversight are handled, reviews high-risk cases, and builds systems that do not depend on one person.

### Proof of work and portfolio

Reviewers should be able to inspect a community strategy and a monthly programming calendar, trace the inputs or decisions behind the work, and understand what the candidate personally owned.

**Strong examples**

- A 30-day community plan.
- A health dashboard with metric caveats.
- An incident postmortem.
- A case study showing feedback turned into a product or process change.

**Weak evidence**

- Follower or member growth with no quality context.
- Event photos without objectives or outcomes.
- Generic engagement calendars copied across projects.

### Common mistakes and misconceptions

**Common mistakes**

- Taking responsibility for the product roadmap and all social-media publishing without the mandate or approval to do so.

**Common misconception**

Community Manager may overlap with Community Moderator, but the hiring evidence is different. This role is judged on community strategy, programming cadence, and moderator oversight, not on ownership of the product roadmap and all social-media publishing.

### Scope boundaries

**Usually owns**

- Community strategy.
- Programming cadence.
- Moderator oversight.
- Feedback systems.
- Community reporting.
- Regional relationship building.

**Usually does not own**

- The product roadmap.
- All social-media publishing.
- Paid acquisition budgets.
- Legal policy.
- Partnership contracts unless explicitly assigned.

### Interview focus

Expect questions about community programme design, reporting and segmentation, and event operations, plus a scenario where the role must handle misinformation, exploit rumors, delayed rewards, unpopular policy changes, regional conflict, and sudden activity spikes. Interviewers are looking for evidence that the candidate knows where community strategy and programming cadence stop and the product roadmap and all social-media publishing begin.

**Example questions**

- How would you measure community health when token speculation drives most activity?
- Describe a time community feedback should change product or policy priorities.
- How would you communicate during an incident when the technical facts are still incomplete?

### Compensation and role risks

Current Web3 listings provide direct evidence for full-time Community Manager roles, but ranges remain geography- and seniority-sensitive. Moderator, local lead, and contractor compensation must be separated.

**Role risks**

- 24/7 expectation creep.
- Being measured on vanity activity.
- Public abuse.
- Responsibility without decision rights.
- Conflicts between regional needs and global messaging.

### Career path and role fit

**Common progression**

- Senior Community Manager.
- Regional or Global Community Lead.
- Ecosystem or Growth Manager.

**This role may fit people who**

Crypto-native operators who like public work, programme building, user feedback, and cross-functional coordination.

**This role may not fit people who**

People who dislike reactive communication, public accountability, or repeated explanation of complex product changes.

### Practical next steps

- Audit one community and identify its actual jobs-to-be-done.
- Build a 30-day programming and reporting system.
- Document how one user signal should move from chat to an internal owner.

## 3. Ambassador Manager

> Designs and operates ambassador programmes that recruit credible advocates, define useful work, control low-quality farming, and measure output beyond signup volume.

### What this role actually does

A typical working day can require the practitioner to review submissions, answer programme questions, coach leads, check evidence, and resolve operational blockers.

The role is accountable for programme structure, applications and selection, and onboarding. It normally hands off decisions about all community operations and all creator partnerships.

### Where the role sits

Ambassador Manager usually sits inside Community, Growth, Ecosystem, or regional market teams. Common reporting lines include Head of Community, Growth Lead, Ecosystem Lead, or a regional market lead. Full-time core-team roles exist, but regional contracts, agencies, contributor programmes, and part-time coverage are common. The role usually collaborates with Community Manager, Creator & Ambassador Partner, Operations Associate, Growth Manager.

### Core responsibilities

- Define programme goals, cohorts, roles, eligibility, and expected output.
- Create application, onboarding, task, review, and reward systems.
- Train local leads and ambassadors on product facts, disclosure, and quality standards.
- Detect duplicate, low-effort, copied, or Sybil-like submissions.
- Resolve reward disputes using documented rules and evidence.
- Report programme output, retention, regional coverage, and downstream value.

### Daily, weekly, and reactive work

**Typical day:** Review submissions, answer programme questions, coach leads, check evidence, and resolve operational blockers.

**Weekly or monthly cadence:** Audit output quality, update tasks, run cohort calls, review rewards, and report performance by region or contribution type.

**When conditions change:** Respond to reward disputes, copied content, fake attendance, identity duplication, policy violations, or a programme task that creates reputational risk.

### Deliverables

- Programme charter.
- Application rubric.
- Onboarding pack.
- Task brief.
- Quality scorecard.
- Reward policy.
- Monthly programme review.

### How success is judged

- Verified useful output.
- Ambassador retention.
- Regional coverage quality.
- Low fraud and dispute rate.
- Clear conversion from activity to education, events, referrals, or product feedback.

Read verified useful output together with ambassador retention. Neither signal is meaningful without the relevant launch, incident, market, workload, or attribution context.

### Tools in practice

- **Notion:** Draft, review, and maintain programme charter and application rubric, with owners, source links, and change history.
- **Airtable:** Track applications, cohorts, evidence, quality reviews, rewards, and disputes across regions.
- **Discord:** Coordinate stakeholders and capture decisions or issues that feed into programme charter and application rubric.
- **CharmVerse or contribution tooling:** Publish programme tasks, collect contribution evidence, review quality, and keep reward decisions auditable.
- **Google Sheets:** Track applications, cohorts, evidence, quality reviews, rewards, and disputes across regions.
- **form tools:** Collect applications and evidence in a consistent format, then route incomplete or suspicious submissions for review.

### Skills and prerequisite knowledge

**Hard skills**

- Programme operations.
- Incentive and quality design.
- Fraud pattern review.
- Community communication.
- Reporting.

**Working skills**

- Clear public communication.
- Calm judgment under pressure.
- Cross-cultural awareness.
- Follow-through.
- Stakeholder expectation management.

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Prior experience in community operations, contributor programmes, regional campaigns, or structured volunteer management is normally more useful than follower count.

### Expectations by level

**Entry level:** At entry level, a candidate should be able to complete a scoped assignment with review. That includes the ability to define programme goals, cohorts, roles, eligibility, and expected output, to create application, onboarding, task, review, and reward systems, and to produce reviewable artifacts such as a programme charter and an application rubric.

**Mid level:** At mid level, the practitioner normally owns programme structure, applications and selection, and onboarding without constant supervision. They can coordinate adjacent teams and improve the workflow behind a programme charter and an application rubric, including when the role must respond to reward disputes, copied content, fake attendance, identity duplication, policy violations, or a programme task that creates reputational risk.

**Senior:** At senior level, the work shifts toward standards, decision rights, and review quality. A senior Ambassador Manager defines how programme structure, applications and selection, and onboarding are handled, reviews high-risk cases, and builds systems that do not depend on one person.

### Proof of work and portfolio

Reviewers should be able to inspect a programme charter and an application rubric, trace the inputs or decisions behind the work, and understand what the candidate personally owned.

**Strong examples**

- A full ambassador programme design.
- An anti-spam and review rubric.
- A cohort tracker.
- A postmortem explaining which incentives produced bad behavior.

**Weak evidence**

- A quest list with no programme objective.
- Signup counts with no proof quality.
- Reward tables that ignore fraud and disputes.

### Common mistakes and misconceptions

**Common mistakes**

- Taking responsibility for all community operations and all creator partnerships without the mandate or approval to do so.

**Common misconception**

Ambassador Manager may overlap with Community Manager, but the hiring evidence is different. This role is judged on programme structure, applications and selection, and onboarding, not on ownership of all community operations and all creator partnerships.

### Scope boundaries

**Usually owns**

- Programme structure.
- Applications and selection.
- Onboarding.
- Task design.
- Quality review.
- Rewards and disputes.
- Regional advocate coordination.

**Usually does not own**

- All community operations.
- All creator partnerships.
- General social strategy.
- Unapproved token promises.
- Broad ecosystem partnership negotiation.

### Interview focus

Expect questions about programme operations, incentive and quality design, and fraud pattern review, plus a scenario where the role must respond to reward disputes, copied content, fake attendance, identity duplication, policy violations, or a programme task that creates reputational risk. Interviewers are looking for evidence that the candidate knows where programme structure and applications and selection stop and all community operations and all creator partnerships begin.

**Example questions**

- How would you stop an ambassador programme becoming a bounty farm?
- What evidence would count for an event, localization task, or educational post?
- How would you handle a regional lead who produces results but repeatedly breaks programme rules?

### Compensation and role risks

Direct public compensation evidence under this exact title is sparse. Use adjacent community-programme evidence and clearly label it.

**Role risks**

- Sybil and copied submissions.
- Reward disputes.
- Regional inconsistency.
- Hidden compliance or disclosure issues.
- Administrative workload overwhelming programme strategy.

### Career path and role fit

**Common progression**

- Senior Community Programs Manager.
- Ecosystem Programs Lead.
- Community or Growth Lead.

**This role may fit people who**

People who enjoy programme design, quality control, distributed coordination, and writing rules that survive edge cases.

**This role may not fit people who**

People who mainly want to create content themselves or who avoid enforcing standards when rewards are involved.

> Sources, briefs, coordinates, and evaluates creators or ambassadors who communicate a product to specific audiences and markets.

### What this role actually does

In practice, the role moves between planned work and live issues. A normal day may require the practitioner to research creators, prepare briefs, answer questions, review drafts, update campaign tracking, and coordinate approvals.

The role is accountable for creator and ambassador relationships, briefing, and campaign coordination. It normally hands off decisions about running the entire ambassador programme and owning all social channels.

### Where the role sits

Creator & Ambassador Partner usually sits inside Community, Growth, Ecosystem, or regional market teams. Full-time core-team roles exist, but regional contracts, agencies, contributor programmes, and part-time coverage are common. The role usually collaborates with Ambassador Manager, Social Media Manager, Product Marketing Manager, Partnerships Manager.

- Identify creators whose audience, language, and format match the campaign.
- Review credibility, historical content, conflicts, fake engagement, and disclosure behavior.
- Negotiate deliverables, timelines, rights, and compensation within approved boundaries.
- Write factual briefs and coordinate product, legal, and brand review.
- Track delivery quality and downstream signals rather than likes alone.
- Maintain long-term relationships and clear records of sponsored work.

### Daily, weekly, and reactive work

**Weekly or monthly cadence:** Review campaign results, refresh the creator pipeline, settle deliverables, and document audience or market learnings.

### Deliverables

- Due-diligence note.
- Campaign brief.

## 42. Market Maker

> Manages liquidity, spreads, execution, inventory, hedging, exchange connectivity, and risk so markets can absorb orders without uncontrolled exposure.

### What this role actually does

This is hands-on work. The role may need to monitor markets and systems, adjust parameters within limits, review fills, and manage exposure.

The boundary matters: it owns liquidity provision, pricing and execution, and inventory risk, not promising token price and protocol marketing.

### Where the role sits

Market Maker usually sits inside Trading, Markets, Treasury, Risk, or quantitative engineering teams. Common reporting lines include Head of Trading, Head of Markets, Chief Investment Officer, or Risk Lead. Usually a specialized full-time role, although liquidity provision may also be delivered by external market-making firms under commercial agreements. The role usually collaborates with DeFi Analyst, Tokenomics Analyst, Backend Engineer, Protocol Researcher.

### Core responsibilities

- Monitor order books, AMMs, spreads, depth, volume, volatility, fees, and inventory.
- Design or operate pricing and execution strategies.
- Manage hedging, exposure, venue limits, and adverse selection.
- Maintain exchange, custody, API, and monitoring infrastructure.
- Review execution quality and market conditions.
- Escalate when models, venues, custody, or risk limits fail.

### Daily, weekly, and reactive work

**Typical day:** Monitor markets and systems, adjust parameters within limits, review fills, and manage exposure.

**Weekly or monthly cadence:** Analyse execution, stress-test assumptions, review venue risk, and update strategy or limits.

**When conditions change:** Respond to extreme volatility, exchange outage, API failure, oracle issue, depeg, custody event, or a model behaving outside expected conditions.

### Deliverables

- Liquidity report.
- Spread and depth analysis.
- Execution dashboard.
- Inventory and risk report.
- Simulation.
- Incident review.

### How success is judged

- Stable execution within mandate.
- Controlled inventory risk.
- Appropriate spreads and depth.
- System reliability.
- Clear limit compliance.

Read stable execution within mandate together with controlled inventory risk. Neither signal is meaningful without the relevant launch, incident, market, workload, or attribution context.

### Tools in practice

- **Python or C++:** Research market behaviour, test pricing or hedging logic, and review execution and risk under defined scenarios.
- **exchange APIs:** Stream order-book and trade data, submit or cancel orders, manage rate limits, and detect venue or connection failures.
- **market data:** Build a consistent view of prices, depth, volatility, fees, funding, and liquidity across venues.
- **risk systems:** Enforce inventory, exposure, loss, venue, and counterparty limits and trigger escalation when a strategy breaches them.
- **Grafana:** Monitor health, latency, errors, resource use, and alert thresholds, then connect incidents to recovery and prevention work.
- **spreadsheets and databases:** Structure the records behind liquidity report and spread and depth analysis and make review status visible.

### Skills and prerequisite knowledge

**Hard skills**

- Market microstructure.
- Statistics.
- Algorithmic execution.
- Risk management.
- Systems and data engineering.

**Working skills**

- Risk discipline.
- Decision-making under uncertainty.
- Clear escalation.
- Numerical skepticism.
- Ability to separate performance from luck.

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Know order books, AMMs, fees, slippage, adverse selection, hedging, venue risk, custody, and the mandate's legal and commercial constraints.

### Expectations by level

**Entry level:** At entry level, a candidate should be able to complete a scoped assignment with review. That includes the ability to monitor order books, AMMs, spreads, depth, volume, volatility, fees, and inventory, to design or operate pricing and execution strategies, and to produce reviewable artifacts such as a liquidity report and spread and depth analysis.

**Mid level:** At mid level, the practitioner normally owns liquidity provision, pricing and execution, and inventory risk without constant supervision. They can coordinate adjacent teams and improve the workflow behind a liquidity report and spread and depth analysis, including when the role must respond to extreme volatility, exchange outage, API failure, oracle issue, depeg, custody event, or a model behaving outside expected conditions.

**Senior:** At senior level, the work shifts toward standards, decision rights, and review quality. A senior Market Maker defines how liquidity provision, pricing and execution, and inventory risk are handled, reviews high-risk cases, and builds systems that do not depend on one person.

### Proof of work and portfolio

Reviewers should be able to inspect a liquidity report and spread and depth analysis, trace the inputs or decisions behind the work, and understand what the candidate personally owned.

**Strong examples**

- A spread and inventory simulation.
- An execution-quality analysis.
- A risk dashboard.
- A market incident postmortem.

**Weak evidence**

- Backtests with no fees or latency.
- Profit screenshots.
- Strategies that assume unlimited liquidity or no risk limits.

### Common mistakes and misconceptions

**Common mistakes**

- Taking responsibility for promising token price and protocol marketing without the mandate or approval to do so.

**Common misconception**

Market Maker may overlap with DeFi Analyst, but the hiring evidence is different. This role is judged on liquidity provision, pricing and execution, and inventory risk, not on ownership of promising token price and protocol marketing.

### Scope boundaries

**Usually owns**

- Liquidity provision.
- Pricing and execution.
- Inventory risk.
- Exchange and venue operations.
- Monitoring.
- Risk limits.

**Usually does not own**

- Promising token price.
- Protocol marketing.
- Treasury strategy generally.
- Exchange listing decisions.
- Uncontrolled proprietary risk.

### Interview focus

Expect questions about market microstructure, statistics, and algorithmic execution, plus a scenario where the role must respond to extreme volatility, exchange outage, API failure, oracle issue, depeg, custody event, or a model behaving outside expected conditions. Interviewers are looking for evidence that the candidate knows where liquidity provision and pricing and execution stop and promising token price and protocol marketing begin.

**Example questions**

- How should spreads change with volatility and inventory?
- What makes apparent liquidity unreliable?
- How would you respond when an exchange connection fails during a fast market?

### Compensation and role risks

**Role risks**

- Bonus-driven incentives.
- Extreme market events.
- Regulatory and counterparty exposure.

**Common progression**

- Senior Market Maker.
- Quant Trading Lead.
- Head of Markets.

**This role may fit people who**

People with strong quantitative, systems, and risk discipline who can distinguish a profitable period from a robust process.

**This role may not fit people who**

People attracted mainly by trading upside or uncomfortable with strict limits and operational controls.
